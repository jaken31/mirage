# Stop the model betting on quiet cells

Gate row 1 fails because the model makes wrong bets on cells where nothing moved. It is not missing any dynamics. The cheapest fix is the one already queued: train on targets where R1's spurious flips are replaced by the previous token. Add an explicit copy gate to make "no change" the default output. Together these should bring quiet-cell accuracy back to a tie with persistence, so the gate is decided by moving cells, where every trained checkpoint already wins. The catch is that the best-case margin is small. Perfect copying on quiet cells plus the best moving-cell skill clears row 1 by about **+0.48 points**, while seeds alone already move the best checkpoint by **0.73 points** (`runs.jsonl` r66, r69). So the gate has to be scored over several seeds with a confidence interval, or a pass will be luck. On the tokenizer side, the project has already proved that GroupNorm causes **all** spurious flips: channel-only normalisation drives them to **0 of 396,013** quiet transitions (`docs/handoff_tokenizer_decision.md`). That tokenizer fails only the 70% token-entropy requirement. A big part of R1's extra entropy is the same global context that makes tokens flip, so the entropy bar itself deserves a second look. If the token route still fails after these fixes, the lowest-risk pivot is a small residual pixel predictor, which has copy-previous-frame built in at zero residual. Delta-IRIS-style conditional tokens are the pivot only if keeping discrete tokens is itself a goal. Throughout, "published" means a cited source states it. "Inference" means the researchers' or this report's reading of how it applies to mirage. Project measurements are cited by `runs.jsonl` row.

## The deficit is wrong bets on quiet fields, already located to the cell

The project's own measurements narrow this down further than any published source does. In r69, the **11.99%** of cells whose 15x15 pixel field moved are scored separately. On those cells persistence gets **50.86%**, and the trained checkpoints get **52.87–54.83%**. That margin grows with training, and the "overfit" final checkpoint is best at **+3.97 points**. On quiet fields the same checkpoints copy only **92.2–95.8%** of tokens, so they change 4–8% of tokens that should have stayed. Against flip-cleaned targets they lose **3.2–6.4 points**. In other words, the quiet-field loss comes from wrong bets, not from unlucky noise (`runs.jsonl` r69). r68 adds the confidence picture. Where the model bets on a change, it claims about 55% confidence and is right about 35% of the time, and half of those cells never changed. Copying unless the model is confident, with the threshold tuned on held-out data, only ties persistence (`runs.jsonl` r68).

Inference: these numbers fit a simple Bayes argument. Suppose a quiet cell flips with probability ε, and a flip lands on one of several neighbouring codes. Then a calibrated model should always predict the previous token, because 1−ε beats any single alternative whenever ε is below one half. A model that changes 4–8% of quiet tokens is claiming that one specific other code is more likely than the old one, and held-out data shows that claim is wrong. Published work explains why plain cross-entropy produces this. Deep nets fit structure first, but they can and do fit noise later ([Arpit et al., ICML 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/arpit17a.html)), and networks trained with cross-entropy tend to be overconfident ([Mukhoti et al., NeurIPS 2020](https://oatml.cs.ox.ac.uk/publications/202009_Mukhoti2020Calibration.html)). Inference: with GroupNorm, a quiet-cell flip is a deterministic function of the *whole* next frame. It is weakly correlated with arm motion elsewhere, so it is learnable on training windows but too weak to generalise. That fits the observed pattern of no clean early-stopping point.

The literature has one structural explanation for why flips exist at all. Per-frame image tokenizers are widely reported to flicker, which is what motivated temporal and 3D tokenizers ([MAGVIT, arXiv 2212.05199](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.05199); [VidTok, arXiv 2412.13061](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.13061v1)). But **no published paper was found that measures a VQ or FSQ tokenizer's flip rate on static regions, or relates it to dynamics accuracy**. Mirage's 8.86% figure for R1 cannot be compared with IRIS, Genie or TinyWorlds, because none of them report one. That gap matters. It means "beat persistence on top-1 token accuracy" is a stricter gate than any major token world model uses. IRIS, Δ-IRIS, Genie, iVideoGPT and TECO report agent return or FVD/PSNR on decoded frames instead ([Δ-IRIS, arXiv 2406.19320](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19320); [iVideoGPT, arXiv 2405.15223](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.15223)).

## Clean targets plus a copy gate, and skip the robust losses

**Target cleaning is the right first move, and the label-noise literature supports the general shape of it.** Published methods split into two families. One fixes the targets or the loss when you know where the noise is. Examples are forward loss correction with a noise transition matrix ([Patrini et al., CVPR 2017, summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/1609.03683)) and bootstrapping ([Reed et al., arXiv 1412.6596](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1412.6596)). The other uses robust losses that limit how hard any one label is fitted, such as GCE ([arXiv 1805.07836](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07836)) and symmetric cross-entropy ([arXiv 1908.06112](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.06112)). Inference: mirage knows exactly where its noise lives (the pixel-quiet mask) and what the clean label is (the previous token). That is the case for the first family, and cleaning is its simplest form. Forward correction says the same thing in principle: train against noisy labels, then evaluate the clean prediction. A mismatch between training targets and evaluation targets is intended in that design, not a leak. Cleaning can lose only one thing. If some quiet-field flips are predictable, for example a code flipping A→B→A, cleaning throws that signal away. Before trusting the result, split quiet cells into "flipped last step" and "quiet whole neighbourhood". That check takes hours.

**A pointer-generator copy gate is the cheap companion.** The published version mixes a generate distribution and a copy distribution with a learned sigmoid gate at each step. It beat the prior abstractive-summarisation state of the art by at least 2 ROUGE ([See et al., ACL 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04368)). Later analysis found the gate's statistics differ between training and testing ([arXiv 2106.01581](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01581)), so its behaviour has to be measured on held-out data. Inference for mirage: add one scalar head per cell, g = σ(w·h). The output becomes p = g·onehot(prev) + (1−g)·softmax(logits), trained with NLL on p. An optional auxiliary BCE loss on g against the pixel-motion mask would use that mask as training-only supervision without touching inputs. At g→1 the model *is* persistence, so it has to "pay" to deviate. The gate alone does not remove the incentive to bet on flips, because raw targets still reward lowering g wherever a flip looks predictable. Gate plus clean targets is the coherent pair: cleaning makes copying the correct answer, and the gate makes it cheap to express. No published paper was found that adds a copy gate to a video or world-model token predictor and reports results against a copy baseline.

**The published evidence and mirage's own runs both argue against the other knobs.** Upweighting changed cells already hurt: at weights 4 and 8 the model predicted change too often (`runs.jsonl` r67). Inference: this matches a known nowcasting failure, where weighted losses buy rare-event recall at the cost of false alarms ([EGU25-19416](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-19416.html)). A false alarm here is exactly a quiet-cell change. Focal loss pushes effort onto hard examples ([Lin et al., arXiv 1708.02002](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02002)), and on raw targets the hard quiet examples are the spurious flips. Robust losses tend to underfit ([arXiv 2002.06482](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.06482)), and they would also damp the gradient on real moving-cell changes, which is where the margin lives. Uniform label smoothing does nothing specific about flips. It also damages the information in the logits ([Müller, Kornblith, Hinton, NeurIPS 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02629v2)). Hard bootstrapping would entrench the model's existing 4–8% quiet flips. The original fixed bootstrap weights do not stop noise fitting in any case ([Arazo et al., ICML 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.11238)). Change weighting or focal loss is worth a try only *on top of* clean targets, to push moving-cell accuracy. Input corruption should stay in. It lowered held-out loss to **0.4592** and delayed overfitting (`runs.jsonl` r67). GameNGen found context noise "critical" for long-rollout stability ([arXiv 2408.14837](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.14837)). Inference: clean targets with noisy inputs teach exactly the invariance the model needs, which is that an input flip is not evidence of motion.

## The tokenizer fix is known, and its only cost is the entropy bar

The research notes proposed a diagnosis: does flip probability depend on distance from motion, or is it flat because GroupNorm makes the leak global? The project ran that test on 2026-08-30. Under GroupNorm, one latent cell's gradient reaches all **4,096 px** of the frame. Under channel-only normalisation it reaches exactly the **225 px** conv field, and the r1c rung shows **0.00%** flips on quiet fields against R1's **8.86%**. The cost: PSNR falls from **31.095 to 30.813 dB**, still above the 30 dB bar, and token entropy falls from **74.1% to 54.6%**, below the 70% bar. Persistence rises to **93.22%**, against R1's **85.67%** (`docs/handoff_tokenizer_decision.md`). The handoff's skew analysis also rules out the obvious entropy remedies on top of r1c. The sum of its channel marginals is already **67.1%**, below 70%, so even a perfect decorrelator would still fail the bar.

Inference: this changes how to read the entropy requirement. The handoff shows that entropy and stability are "the same knob": GroupNorm lets the same 8x8 patch take different codes depending on the rest of the frame. So the roughly 20 points of entropy R1 has over r1c are partly *made of* spurious flips. That is information a dynamics model cannot use and is punished for chasing. A 70% bar set before this was known may be rewarding the wrong thing. The most valuable cheap move on the tokenizer side may be a requirements decision, not training: either restate the entropy floor for a tokenizer whose encoder respects its receptive field, or measure entropy only over information that a local field can determine. Any such change should go through `docs/world_model_requirements.md` and the decision record, since it reopens a closed Phase 1 choice and moves `tokenizer_hash`.

If the entropy bar stays, three tokenizer variants remain, ranked from cheapest. **First, the K=3 local-normalisation rung.** It is the only in-between point the three-stride architecture allows, with a 1,849 px field, and it has not been measured. Inference: flips would fall a lot but not to zero, and entropy would land somewhere between the two existing rungs. That makes it the one point on the curve that might clear both bars. **Second, a margin loss on the bounded FSQ latent**, fine-tuned from R1. The published basis is quantization-aware training of network weights, where weights "oscillate between two grid-points". Nagel et al.'s fix, "oscillation dampening", pulls values toward bin centres, and with it they report state-of-the-art 3–4-bit accuracy ([Nagel et al., ICML 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/nagel22a.html)). Inference: this only shrinks the share of latents near rounding boundaries. Because GroupNorm's perturbation is global and systematic, a margin loss reduces flips without eliminating them, and pulling latents to bin centres risks skewing the code distribution, which is the very term that is already short. **Third, a pixel-diff freeze or hysteresis at tokenization time.** If a cell's 15x15 field is bit-identical to the previous frame, reuse the previous token. Inference: r1c's zero shows the freeze is exact for this renderer. But tokens then depend on the previous frame, and the R1 decoder was never trained on frames assembled from codes of different frames, so measure PSNR before relying on it. No source was found that uses temporal hysteresis in a VQ/FSQ tokenizer. The nearest published idea is reusing earlier-frame tokens at the model level ([ITC, arXiv 2605.16457](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.16457v3)).

Training-time noise injection and a static-cell latent-consistency loss (pairs of adjacent frames, penalising latent change on quiet cells) are both plausible. Inference: both are dominated here, because local normalisation already gets to zero flips. They matter only if global context has to stay in the encoder.

## Score the gate the way video prediction learned to

**Published precedent strongly supports scoring moving and quiet regions separately, while keeping the copy baseline.** Mathieu et al. computed PSNR only where optical flow exceeded a threshold, because it "is trivial to predict pixel values in static areas". In those areas the copy baseline (28.6 dB) beat some of their models ([arXiv 1511.05440](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05440)). MCnet lost to copy-last-frame overall on UCF101 and then reported motion-masked scores plus motion-decile buckets ([Villegas et al., ICLR 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08033)). Villegas et al. later wrote that per-frame evaluations "are not reliable when a large portion of a video does not move", and kept copy-last-frame as a reference curve ([NeurIPS 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01655)). Meteorology formalises the same idea as a skill score relative to persistence, and warns that it "can be unstable for small sample sizes" ([JWGFVR](https://jwgfvr.github.io/forecastverification)).

**Changed-only scoring has a known trap, and the fix is a second number.** Scoring only events is the critical success index, which is "the accuracy when correct negatives have been removed". It rewards over-forecasting change, so forecasters pair it with a bias score or false-alarm rate ([JWGFVR](https://jwgfvr.github.io/forecastverification)). Inference: a defensible restated gate has two parts, both against persistence on the same windows. First, on pixel-moving cells, model accuracy must beat persistence. Second, on pixel-quiet cells, the rate of predicting a token different from the previous one must stay under a bound fixed in advance. Equivalently, accuracy on flip-cleaned quiet targets must be at least persistence's. This keeps the principle the project restated on 2026-09-18, that a gate a zero-parameter baseline can pass shows nothing. Avoid a single ratio skill score: on flip-cleaned quiet cells persistence error is zero, so the ratio blows up.

**The thin margin makes statistics part of the gate.** Inference, from the project's numbers: if cleaning works perfectly, the raw row-1 margin is about 0.12 times the moving-cell margin, which is **+0.24 to +0.48 points**. The r69 upper estimate assumes the best moving-cell skill survives cleaning. That whole range sits inside the 0.73-point seed spread. Report row 1 over at least three seeds, with an episode-level bootstrap interval, and the motion-decile breakdown. Test-label noise is known to reorder model rankings ([Northcutt et al., arXiv 2103.14749](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14749)), which is a further reason to report raw and flip-cleaned scores side by side. A pixel-based "spurious" criterion does not depend on the model, so it is the token analogue of the optical-flow mask, not post-hoc label editing. Two further additions are inference with no published precedent for token world models. One is NLL against a *probabilistic* persistence baseline (copy with probability 1−ε, ε fitted on train), which credits calibration that top-1 accuracy ignores. The other is a coherence horizon: the rollout step at which moving-field skill falls to the copy baseline, following the per-horizon curves TECO and GameNGen report ([TECO, arXiv 2210.02396](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02396); [GameNGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.14837)).

## If tokens still lose, predict the residual instead

**The field's main answer to mostly-static scenes is architectural, not a training trick.** Δ-IRIS conditions both encoder and decoder on past frames and actions. It uses 4 tokens per frame against IRIS's 16–64, with a 3-layer, 512-wide transformer on one A100, and reaches Atari 100k mean HNS **1.39** against IRIS's **1.05**. Without its continuous per-frame "I-tokens", its Crafter score fell from **42.47 to 25.92** ([Δ-IRIS, arXiv 2406.19320](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19320)). iVideoGPT's decoder ablation reproduced the motion but lost the scene, which shows its conditional tokens carry only dynamics ([arXiv 2405.15223](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15223v3)). DIAMOND avoids tokens entirely. Its roughly 4M-parameter frame-stacked U-Net with EDM diffusion reached HNS **1.459**, on one RTX 4090 at about 12 GB VRAM, and its authors attribute frame-to-frame inconsistency in IRIS to IRIS's discrete tokens ([arXiv 2405.12399](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12399v2)). Action-conditioned pixel-motion models have worked on robot arms since Finn et al.'s 59k-interaction pushing dataset ([NeurIPS 2016](https://proceedings.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2016/file/d9d4f495e875a2e075a1a4a6e1b9770f-Paper.pdf)). SimVP-style CNN predictors are cheap on one GPU ([arXiv 2206.05099](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.05099)).

Inference, for an 8 GB laptop and a two-week phase: the lowest-risk pivot is a deterministic residual pixel predictor that outputs x_{t−1} + Δ from the last k frames and the action, at SimVP or small-U-Net size. Copy-previous-frame sits in its function class at zero output, and an action-conditioned MuJoCo arm is close to deterministic. Upgrade to DIAMOND-style EDM on the same backbone only if outputs blur at contacts. Fitting 8 GB probably means a smaller batch or bf16, which is unverified. Δ-IRIS with FSQ swapped in for its VQ is untested. It is about a week of work, it replaces the tokenizer outright, and Δ-IRIS itself reports that predicting delta tokens is harder. Choose it only to keep the discrete-token design. Slot models (SOLD) and iVideoGPT-scale pretraining are out of budget: SAVi pretraining alone took about 1M frames ([SOLD, arXiv 2410.08822](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08822v2)), and iVideoGPT's tokenizer took 17 GPU-days. MaskGIT dynamics were already ruled out because they would change the attention mask, an irreversible decision (`docs/research_copy_collapse.md`).

## Conclusion

The question has moved from "why does the model lose" to "is row 1 measuring the right thing". The project has already shown that the tokenizer's global normalisation is the whole cause of the flips, and that the model wins wherever pixels actually move. What is left is mostly measurement and requirements work, not model work. Clean the targets, gate the copy, and score with enough seeds that a margin under half a point means something. The entropy bar is the sharpest open question. It currently rewards R1 for exactly the global context that produces the noise the dynamics model is punished for chasing. Revisiting it may beat any amount of training-side cleverness.

The deeper lesson is that none of the published token world models would have failed this way in public, because none of them gate on top-1 token accuracy against copy. Mirage's gate is stricter than the field's norm. That strictness has already exposed a tokenizer property the literature never measures. If the cleaned run still cannot clear the bar across seeds, that is strong evidence that per-frame tokens are the wrong representation for a mostly-static arm scene. The residual pixel predictor is then a better bet than further work on the tokenizer.
